## Part 4a — lesson time 0:00 to 7:42
Recording: `duomo-lessons/4a.mp4` (4:13; the first ~22 seconds are the page before playback starts)

### The missing piece in trading education
- **Making logically justified decisions isn't enough on its own. A real skill must be measurable and improvable.**
- If you **can't separate the quality of your judgment from the outcome of a trade**, you're leaving consistent profitability to luck. (Slide: *Measure judgement separately to improve deliberately.*)

### Two traders, last 30 trades
- **Trader A:** **+18%**, a steady equity curve, the kind of run posted on social media with a motivational caption.
- **Trader B:** **−4%**.
- One has a genuine edge; the other is effectively gambling. Asked which to learn from, copy or be, **everyone picks Trader A.**
- **Behind the returns:**
  - **Trader A** takes **1:3** trades whose actual probability is **25%**, exactly the **base rate** for 1:3. **No advantage, expectancy of zero.** The positive results are **just luck**, the same as entries picked by coin flip (Lesson 2).
  - **Trader B** takes **1:1** trades with a **55%** probability against a **50%** base rate. **Positive expectancy, a real edge that compounds over time.** B has simply **hit a completely normal losing patch.**
  - *(Fixed ratios are used only to keep the example simple. In real trading the ratio is dictated by the context.)*

### The part to get your head around
- Run the maths across 30 trades for many traders like these:
  - **About 1 in 4 traders with B's genuine edge will be showing a loss.**
  - **About half of the no-edge, coin-flip traders like A will be showing a profit** (slide labels this "after 50 trades").
- **Half the pure gamblers look skilled, and a quarter of the genuinely skilled look like failures.** (Slide: *Small sample sizes can give misleading results*, "1 in 4 lose" vs "1 in 2 profit".)
- So **your last 30 trades can't tell you which trader you are.** It isn't that the results *struggle* to tell you. **They cannot tell you. The information isn't there.**
- It's Lesson 1's 200 random strategies again, but now aimed at **your own account**, where you draw conclusions about yourself that the data can't support.

### The law of small numbers
- A cognitive bias: believing **a small sample represents** what you'd get from a much larger one.
  - Five losses feel like proof something's wrong; a profitable month feels like proof you've figured it out. **Neither can tell you that.**
- **P&L matters over a large enough sample, but over a short period it can be completely dominated by noise.** Use that noise to judge your skill and it steers your confidence, your decisions and your changes, **potentially in the wrong direction.**

### The improvement loop that goes nowhere
- In a losing period you ask what went wrong, but **your P&L and your journal can't tell you** whether the problem was:
  - your **market analysis** (the opportunity you decided was there),
  - your **execution**,
  - your **trade-management actions**,
  - or just **normal variance** you'd expect with probabilities.
- So you **change something tangible**: market, timeframe, entry criteria, risk. Results improve, and you assume **the last change was the fix**.
- Then results turn bad again and you're **back to guessing** what to change this time.
- It **feels like refining** your approach, but you're **guessing which change caused which outcome** and letting the next run of results confirm it or not.
- Traders spend **years in this loop without improving**, paying through courses, **funded-account challenge fees**, lost capital, and time and effort they never get back. He argues failing this way often **costs more than paying for serious development**; it's just paid in **less noticeable installments**.

### The dark-room free throws
- Practicing basketball free throws in a **pitch-black room**: you can't see the hoop or the ball's flight. **Every 100 shots, someone tells you how many went in.**
- Change your technique; if the score goes up, keep the change; if it goes down, try another.
- **You can't connect action to effect, so the score teaches you nothing.** You could put in years of time and effort without improving.
- **That dark room is how most traders operate:** the score is the P&L, and everything that produces it is invisible.

### Turning the lights on: measure the estimates
- If P&L can't tell you what to improve, what can?
- From Lesson 2, the central skill that everything else feeds or acts on is **estimating the probabilities of potential outcomes accurately.**
- **So the first thing to measure is the accuracy of those estimates.**

### Calibration: the weather-forecaster example
- A forecaster says **70% chance of rain**. On that one day it either rains or it doesn't, so one day can't test the number.
- **You test it across all the occasions they said 70%.** Did it rain about 70% of those times?
  - **Yes** → they're **well calibrated**.
  - If it rained **only 40%** of those times → their **70% estimate is inaccurate on average**. *(The recording ends here, at lesson time 7:42.)*

### Key takeaways from Part 4a
- **Measure judgment separately from outcomes**, or improvement is left to luck.
- **30 trades can't reveal skill:** about half of zero-edge traders profit, and about a quarter of real-edge traders lose.
- **Law of small numbers:** short-run P&L is mostly noise. Don't let it steer your confidence or your changes.
- **The P&L/journal loop can't separate analysis, execution, management and variance**, so changes become guesses credited to luck.
- **The dark room:** a score with no visible cause can't teach you anything.
- **The first thing to measure is how accurate your probability estimates are: calibration**, tested across many forecasts at the same stated probability.
