### Where the series has landed
- You want to know that the time you're putting in is getting you **closer to consistent profitability.** The series has looked at why that probably hasn't happened; this lesson shows **how his approach addresses those problems and how the full trading process works.**

### Starting point: trading is a skill
- **Having a strategy**, your own or someone else's, **doesn't mean you've developed the skills you need.** A strategy gives conditions to look for and actions to take when they appear.
- But **every situation has its own context.** The same setup can appear in two situations **with different probabilities**, so the right decision can be completely different.
- **The phrase-book analogy:** you can memorize phrases perfectly, but if you don't understand what someone's saying to you, how do you choose the right response? Sometimes you'll get it right, but that doesn't mean you can hold a conversation. **In trading you must understand each situation well enough to decide how to act.** Checking it against a predefined list of conditions doesn't give you that.

### Layer 1: the Duomo market theory
- **The basis of his trading throughout his career.** It came out of building an **algorithmic trading system** with a team of specialists: investigating **how the market works**, **why price moves the way it does**, **which parts of movement can be forecast and which can't**, and **how to identify price levels where the activity driving a move is likely to change, and how it will change.**
- What the theory explains:
  - the difference between activity **driven by new information** that changes valuations, and activity that **follows the market's own inner mechanics**;
  - **how price movements relate across timeframes**, and why **particular levels** help identify changes in that activity.
- That gives the analysis a basis **beyond "I've seen this pattern work before."** You can explain **why** you're paying attention to certain things and **what they tell you about the context.**

### Layer 2: the Duomo methods (applying the theory)
- **Interpret all relevant contextual elements**: **price structure**, **significant levels**, and **the activity around them**.
- **Connect that information across timeframes** to assess the full context.
- **The aim isn't more confirmations. It's working out the potential outcomes and accurately estimating their probabilities.**
- **His challenge:** you may already look at several timeframes and analyze movement around levels, but **does it lead to a probability estimate you can measure and improve, or just to a decision that a setup looks strong enough?**
  - **Without an estimate you can't compare against the base rate**, and that comparison is how you find an edge.

### The edge, stated precisely
- **Base rate** = the **breakeven probability**: the probability if there were no advantage at all in that situation.
- **Example:** a **1:3** trade has a breakeven (base rate) of **25%**. If your forecast puts the probability at **35%**, you've identified **positive expectancy.** (Slide: *The difference between the base rate and the actual probability is the edge.*)
- **You're not deciding which outcome is more likely. You're identifying that difference. That is the edge in each situation.**
- That's how you move from **an opinion** about what happens next to **real, logical reasons to take a trade.**

### Strategy comes after the forecast
- Once you have a forecast, you decide **how to trade that particular opportunity.** **Your strategy has to reflect the context you analyzed and the forecast you made.**
- **Every action is a statement** about the opportunity and context:
  - **Entering** says there's **positive expectancy now.**
  - **Your stop** says **where the advantage is no longer there** and you can't justify holding.
  - **Your risk size** should reflect **the level of expectancy**, the quality of the opportunity, within your risk limits.
- **A major problem for most traders:** if your actions don't match your forecast, **you aren't trading the opportunity your analysis identified.** You can analyze well and still decide badly, **turning a positive-expectancy opportunity into a negative one.**

### Managing the trade: keep forecasting
- The strategy for each trade must cover **both the entry and how you'll manage it as the context changes and the position evolves.**
- **Once you're in, keep analyzing the context and updating your forecast.** Only that tells you whether to **hold, scale out, scale in, move the stop, or exit.**
- **The fixed-target mistake:** setting a profit target at entry and exiting automatically when price hits it.
  - Reaching the target only tells you **the move you originally forecast has happened.** It doesn't tell you **whether there's still an opportunity from that point onward.**
  - **Reassess the next potential outcomes and probabilities.** If holding still has **positive expectancy**, you have **no reason to exit**; if not, you have a reason to exit.
  - The same assessment can tell you to **exit *before* the original target** if the opportunity is gone. There's no reason to hold for an **arbitrary target set at entry.**
- **You aren't guessing whether price will keep going. You're deciding whether the probability, compared with the base rate, is high enough to justify keeping the position open.** Each decision reflects **the opportunity as it is now**, not as it was at entry.

### Using your trading data: calibration
- With trading data **tracked and used correctly**, you can:
  - **compare your probability estimates with how often the outcomes actually happened**;
  - see **where your forecasts are more or less accurate**, including **how that changes with different elements of the market context.**
- That lets you separate **three different causes**, each with its own fix:
  1. **Calibration problem**: you **consistently estimate too high or too low** for a particular contextual element or type of situation → **adjust your estimates.**
  2. **Skill gap**: you aren't reading the chart or interpreting parts of the context well enough, or aren't making the right logical decisions → **develop that skill.**
  3. **Outside your control**: no mistake or skill gap to fix → **accept it.**
- **Treat them all as the same problem and you'll spend time and effort changing the wrong things**, maybe making things worse.

### The framework: the Cohesion Trading Model (CTM)
- He calls this the **Cohesion Trading Model**, "the optimal operating system for traders." It covers the **full process: selecting a market → analyzing the situation → forecasting → planning a strategy → managing trades → improving performance through data.**
- **Most common trading issues, including most psychology problems, happen when traders are out of alignment with this model.** Aligned, it **stops those problems emerging** and lets you **perform at your highest potential and improve over time.**
- **The stack:**
  - **CTM**: the operating model that directs how you use analysis, make decisions and improve performance.
  - **Duomo market theory**: explains market behavior.
  - **Duomo methods**: how the market gets analyzed.

### The trading "flywheel"
- A **flywheel** is a cycle where **each turn improves the next**, so improvement can compound over time.
- In trading: **better analysis → better forecasts → better decisions → your trades and data show where to improve analysis and decision-making → repeat.** Each cycle **improves your edge and how effectively you trade it.**
- It solves the core problem of searching for fixes without knowing what needs fixing: you can **direct your time and effort** and **measure the difference** each change makes.

### The four roles of successful trading
Every trader already performs these roles, but usually **mashes them together**, so each is done **sub-optimally**. Each needs a **different skill set and mode of thinking.**
1. **Analyst**: **gather market information objectively.** See what's actually happening, **without favoring information that supports a position you've already decided you want.**
2. **Investigator**: **interpret** the information. Connect the dots in the context, **identify potential outcomes, estimate their probabilities → produce a forecast.**
3. **Strategist**: if the forecast shows **positive expectancy**, **decide how to act on it**, covering both the **entry** and the **ongoing strategy**, so your actions logically match the analysis.
4. **Calibrator**: over time, **use your trading data** to identify **adjustments, skill gaps, and areas to improve.**
- **Why separate them:** you focus on the right task at the right time. Gathering information isn't the same as deciding what it means, and **estimating a probability isn't the same as choosing how to trade it.**
- It also makes development **specific**: you can see **which role needs work and which skill within it**, and **progress through the roles in order**, starting by learning to execute the **analyst** role correctly, then the **investigator**, and so on.

### A professional trading operating system needs five things together
1. A way to **analyze the unique context** of each situation and understand what's happening.
2. A way to turn that analysis into **an interpretation and a forecast**: potential outcomes plus probability estimates.
3. A way to choose **logical actions that reflect the forecast**, at entry and throughout the trade.
4. A way to **use trading data to keep improving**: the flywheel.
5. **The professional mindset and psychology** to apply all of it **consistently**, every session.
- **Knowing what to do isn't enough if you can't do it consistently, and consistency isn't enough without a way to find and fix your weaknesses.** For trading you can depend on, develop all of these parts together.

### Key takeaways from Part 5a
- **A strategy is a phrase book; trading needs fluency**: understanding each unique context well enough to choose the right action.
- **Edge = estimated probability minus base rate (breakeven).** For example, 35% against a 25% breakeven on a 1:3 trade.
- **Analysis must end in a measurable probability estimate**, not "the setup looks strong."
- **Every action is a statement:** entry (positive expectancy now), stop (where the edge disappears), size (how big the edge is).
- **Keep forecasting while in a trade.** Exit when the expectancy is gone, not because a fixed target was hit, and hold past the target if the edge remains.
- **Data → calibration** separates three causes: **mis-estimated probabilities, skill gaps, and things outside your control**, each with its own fix.
- **Four roles: Analyst → Investigator → Strategist → Calibrator.** Keep them separate and develop them in order.
- **Five parts of a professional operating system:** context analysis, forecast, aligned actions, the data flywheel, and consistent psychology.
