## Part 3a
Recording: `duomo-lessons/3a.mp4` (2:44)

### The premise
- Consistent profitability requires **real trading skill, not better strategy-following.**
- That skill isn't only about analyzing a market. **You need a way to make decisions that are logical enough for your brain to trust.** Without it, you're risking money on judgments that have **no convincing basis**.

### The coin game
- A **tested, perfectly fair coin**: exactly 50% heads, 50% tails.
- **Rules:** heads, *you pay me £10*; tails, *I pay you £20*.
- That's an **average profit of £5 per flip for you**, a clear **positive-expectancy** opportunity.
- The first **five flips all land heads**. You've lost five in a row and you're **£50 down**. Do you keep playing?
  - **Of course.** You know the opportunity is still in your favor, and five losses in a row can happen.
  - You **wouldn't hesitate**, wouldn't **cut the amount at risk**, wouldn't wonder whether you're "cut out for coin flipping." You'd keep playing until you'd taken every penny.

### Now compare that to your trading
After five losses in a row in your trading:
- you start **hesitating at entries**,
- you **cut your risk** per trade,
- you **close profitable trades too early**, to claw back some losses and because you're scared of losing the profit you've built.

### What that means
- The coin flip and your trading are **both still probabilistic**, but your **behavior** is completely different in trading.
- **The difference isn't discipline or trading psychology.**
  - **In the coin game you know the probabilities, and you have proof the edge is real.**
  - **In your trading you may have neither.**
- So when losses happen, **self-doubt isn't necessarily a psychology flaw. It may simply be the rational response to having no evidence.**

### What you're actually trying to develop
- **The stability and reassurance you'd feel in the coin game is very close to what you're trying to build in trading.**
- Then you can take five losses in a row with real money gone, and **the losses haven't destroyed the logic of the next decision.**
  - No motivational speech needed.
  - No need to **rebuild your confidence** before the next trade, just as you wouldn't before the next coin flip.
  - **You still understand the situation and the expectancy.**
- That's what changes when your forecasts and trade decisions are justified… *(continues in 3b)*

### Key takeaways from Part 3a
- **The same losing streak produces opposite behavior** depending on whether you *know* your edge is real.
- **Hesitation, cutting size and early exits after losses may be rational reactions to missing evidence**, not character flaws.
- **The goal is coin-game stability:** losses don't break the logic of the next decision because the edge is understood and proven.

## Part 3b
Recording: `duomo-lessons/3b.mp4` (4:50)

### Stability comes from evidence, not personality
- Instead of spending the rest of the week trying to rebuild your confidence, **your next decision is still supported by logic and by your own trading data.** (Slide: **Logic + data + evidence**.)
- **That stability isn't a personality trait.** You won't create it by reading more about mindset and psychology. **It develops partly when your analysis and trading data give your brain a justified basis for action.**
- **You can't talk yourself into evidence your trading has never produced.**

### The "easy culprits"
- Traders are always told to **fix their mindset, master their emotions, become emotionally neutral, be more disciplined**.
- Those are **the easy things to blame**: if the problem is always your psychology, it can never be the useless trading approach someone taught you.
- Worse, **much trading-psychology advice conflicts with what neuroscience and evidence-based psychology say is actually happening.**

### Risk vs. ambiguous uncertainty (the neuroscience)
- Neuroscience distinguishes:
  - **Risk**: you have some idea of the outcomes and probabilities.
  - **Ambiguous uncertainty**: information about the probabilities is **unknown or missing**.
- **The brain treats them very differently.** Ambiguity creates **greater conflict** and recruits **stronger vigilance and evaluation responses**, especially when money is at stake: survival instincts and fear responses.
- **The average trader:** no base rates, no explicit probability estimates, no expectancy, no reliable evidence of an edge. Their decisions are **closer to ambiguous uncertainty**, because the analysis gives the brain **nothing it can resolve**.
- So it's no surprise that **anxiety and impulsive decisions take over.** The brain is responding to the decision it's actually been given. **The problem is what's being given to the brain.**
- **Hesitation and panic exits blamed on discipline may be the predictable response** to risking money where the probability and logic haven't been established.

### The explore/exploit trap
- We evolved an **explore/exploit** system for uncertain situations: the brain **searches until it finds a certain, reliable resource, then settles on it and exploits it.**
- **Markets never provide that certainty**, so the search can go on forever and you **never reach the exploit phase.**
- It shows up as **another indicator, another confluence, another opinion, another confirmation.** It looks like thoroughness, but it's **your brain's loop searching for something the market can't give you.**
- **The fix isn't more practice forcing yourself through the same experience. It's changing the kind of decision your brain is asked to make.**

### Change the question
- **"Will this trade make money? Is this what the market will do next?"** is **unanswerable**. No amount of staring at charts, adding confirmations or doing more analysis resolves it, so your brain keeps searching and you never feel able to settle on an opportunity.
- **A professional trader instead:** defines the **possible outcomes** → **estimates their probabilities** → **chooses an action that follows logically.**
- That **moves the decision from ambiguity toward a measurable risk.**
- Producing those forecasts is a **skill**. It depends on analyzing the right way, estimating probabilities, and **calibration** (how accurate you are). You **can't manufacture it** through confidence exercises or reading about trading psychology.

### A trading plan doesn't fix it either
- You can write down entry rules, risk limits and a trade-management checklist perfectly.
- If the analysis behind it has **never produced a justified forecast**, you've got **a more organized version of an unsupported decision.**
- **A plan can make your actions more consistent. It can't make the reasoning behind them valid.**

### The "remove your emotions" myth
- The most repeated psychology advice in trading: **trade like a robot, be emotionally neutral.** He calls it complete nonsense: not just unhelpful but **scientifically impossible**.
  - **Emotion is necessary for decision-making.** People who lose emotional processing through brain injury don't become perfectly rational; **they struggle to make any decisions at all** (well documented).
  - **Emotions carry information you haven't consciously processed.**
  - Research on investors and experienced traders **doesn't show the best performers feel nothing.** It shows they **understand what they're feeling, use that information more effectively, and recover more efficiently.**
- **Emotional neutrality should never have been the goal. The goal is to create conditions for logical decision-making**, which partly depends on whether your analysis leaves you guessing or gives you **a measurable risk you can reason about.**

### Re-reading common "psychology" problems
- **Hesitation** may come from **missing evidence**, not missing discipline.
- **Overtrading** may come from an approach that never gives you a **logical reason to take a trade or not**, so you rely on feelings.
- **Closing winners early** may come from having **no logical action for the next step that relates back to the context**, which leaves feelings as the only reason to act.
- So **calling all of these fear, discipline or emotional problems isn't really a diagnosis.**
  - **Some are produced or intensified by the trading process itself.**
  - **Others come from deeper psychological, physiological or learned mechanisms** and need a different solution.
  - Lump them together and you can't know which problem you're treating, or whether the treatment makes sense.

### Vocabulary isn't diagnosis
- You can know all about **loss aversion, dopamine, cognitive biases, emotional regulation** and still repeat the same behavior, because you can't identify **the mechanism operating in that moment.**
- **Knowing the name of a bias isn't the same as being able to:** diagnose what caused the decision → prevent it where possible → use an appropriate method to change the pattern that remains.

- In the coin game, decisions felt different because you had **proof of your edge.** **Where does the equivalent evidence come from in trading?**

### Key takeaways from Part 3b
- **Trading stability = logic + data + evidence**, not a personality trait or a mindset you can read your way into.
- **Risk vs. ambiguous uncertainty:** without base rates, probabilities and expectancy, your brain treats trades as ambiguity, and **anxiety and impulsive decisions are the predictable result.**
- **The endless confluence hunt is explore/exploit stuck in "explore."** Fix it by changing the question from "will this make money?" to outcomes → probabilities → logical action.
- **A plan makes actions consistent, not valid.**
- **"Remove emotions" is impossible and wrong.** Use emotions as information and create conditions for logical decisions.
- **"Fear / discipline / emotions" isn't a diagnosis.** Some problems come from the process, some from deeper mechanisms; knowing bias names doesn't identify the mechanism.
