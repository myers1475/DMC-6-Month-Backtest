## Part 2a — lesson time ~0:08 to 7:40
Recording: `duomo-lessons/Lesson2a.mp4` (3:49, played at about 2x)

### Setup for the lesson
- Picks up from Lesson 1: you become consistently profitable by developing the real skills of trading, not by finding a strategy to follow.
- This session sets out to identify **the central skill at the heart of trading** by answering one question: **what decision is a trader actually making?**

### The thought experiment: trading blind
- "We trade together, but you can't see the charts." He picks **random entry times** and **flips a coin** for long or short. **Your only decision is whether to take each trade.**
- **Trade 1:** risk 50 pips to make 50 (**1:1**). Heads, so it's a long. Take it?
  - Hopefully **no**, unless you fancy a pure gamble.
  - Why not? It isn't that you think a loss is *more* likely; if you did, you'd just take the other side. It's that **with no view of the chart, you have no idea whether there's an advantage in either direction.** At 1:1 that's 50% loss / 50% profit, so there's nothing to gain, and you pass.
- **Trade 2:** same setup, but **1:3**, risking 50 pips to make 150. This is the "asymmetric risk-reward" traders are always told to look for, because you can lose more often and still make money.
  - Some people get pulled in by the ratio and say yes. **The rational answer is still no.**
  - If a ratio alone created an advantage, you could take random trades all day and print money, and you know you can't.
  - **With a target 3× further than the stop, random price movement reaches it about 25% of the time.** The bigger wins are cancelled out by the more frequent losses. The same holds for any ratio: **change the ratio and the probabilities change with it.**
- **Conclusion:** with no analysis (no chart), there's **no advantage**.

### Key term: **base rate**
- **Base rate** = the no-advantage probability: what you'd expect from **random movement** between the outcomes. (At 1:1 it's 50/50; at 1:3, roughly 25% to the target.)

### First "undeniable truth"
> **Risk-reward ratios don't matter in isolation.** None of them carries any advantage on its own.

So if the ratio isn't where the advantage comes from, **what changes when you can see the charts** and decide from your own analysis?

### What every trade is really saying
- In a normal session you do your analysis, find a 1:3 opportunity, and decide to take it.
- Whether you realize it or not, **you're making a statement: the probability of a profitable outcome is higher than the base rate.**
  - If you thought it was just the base rate, there's no advantage, so you'd pass (as in the game).
  - If you thought it was *worse* than the base rate, you'd pass or trade the other direction.
- **Every trade you've ever taken has made that statement.**

### His definition of trading
> **Trading is forecasting the financial markets to find situations where an outcome's probability is better than the base rate, then taking actions that logically align with those outcomes and probabilities.**

### "Trading is a game of probabilities", yet probabilities rarely appear
- The industry loves saying this, but watch how most traders actually work and **probabilities usually play no part in their analysis or decision-making.**
- At most they'll quote a **success rate** or call something a **"high-probability setup."** **That isn't the same thing:**
  - A **success rate** tells you what **percentage of a group of past trades** ended in profit.
  - A **probability estimate** is **a judgment about defined outcomes in the specific, unique situation in front of you.**

### What a proper probability estimate requires
1. Know the **base rate**.
2. Understand the **current context**.
3. Be able to **explain why the information you're analyzing significantly changes your judgment** of the probabilities, so that you end up with an estimate **meaningfully different from the base rate**.

*Slide example ("A probability estimate is a judgement about this unique situation"):*
| | Outcome A (profit) | Outcome B (loss) |
|---|---|---|
| Distance | 100 pips | 50 pips |
| Base rate | 33.33% | 66.67% |
| Estimated probability | **42%** | **58%** |
→ marked as a **valid opportunity**, because the estimate for the profit outcome is above its base rate.

- If your analysis ends with "this looks good" or "this seems profitable," **you haven't made a probability judgment. You've described a confidence level.** You haven't shown **where** the advantage in that situation comes from, or **how much** advantage there is.

### The answer: the central skill
> **The one skill successful trading is built on is being able to estimate the probabilities of potential outcomes.**
> That's the foundation. **Everything else in trading either feeds that skill or acts on it.**

### Ratio vs. probability: two trades
- **Trade A:** 1:3 (asymmetric), but **estimated probability of profit 23%**, which is *below* its base rate (~25%). Repeated over time, **it loses money.**
- **Trade B:** 1:1 (symmetrical), with **estimated probability of profit 55%**, far enough *above* its 50% base rate to **make money.**
- The supposedly better asymmetric ratio is **the worst opportunity.**
- **What separates them is expectancy.**

### Key term: **expectancy**
- Expectancy tells you whether **the probabilities and outcomes of an opportunity combine to produce a genuine advantage over time.**
  - **Positive** → the opportunity has an advantage.
  - **Negative** → taking trades like that will **drain your account** over time.
- He deliberately **doesn't make this an expectancy tutorial**. Knowing the equation isn't the hard part. **The hard part is producing accurate probability estimates you can actually believe in and trust.**
- If the number going into the calculation is just a feeling (say you decided to call it "40% for, 60% against"), the expectancy calculation **ends up meaningless**.
- **The gap in most trading education:** it teaches you to calculate expectancy **without teaching you how to produce a probability estimate you can trust.**

### Not a case for 1:1 trades
- He isn't telling you to trade 1:1. In another situation, 1:3 might be the better opportunity.
- **There is no magic risk-reward ratio.** The ratio of each opportunity is **dictated by the context**… *(the recording ends here, at lesson time 7:40)*

### Key takeaways from Part 2a
- **Base rate** = the probability from random movement alone. **No analysis means no edge**, at any risk-reward ratio.
- **Risk-reward ratios carry no edge on their own.** A farther target just means a lower base-rate chance of reaching it.
- **Every trade implicitly claims the profit probability is above the base rate.**
- **Trading = forecasting situations where an outcome beats the base rate, then acting in line with it.**
- **A success rate isn't a probability estimate.** The estimate is a judgment about *this* situation: base rate + context + a reason the evidence moves the odds.
- **The core skill is estimating the probabilities of potential outcomes**; everything else feeds it or acts on it.
- **Expectancy combines probability and payoff**, but it's only as good as the probability you feed it.

### The ratio comes from context
- The ratio of each opportunity is **dictated by the context of that unique situation and by what the meaningful potential outcomes are.**
- **On its own, the ratio tells you nothing.**

### What most traders call "analysis"
- Most "analysis," he says, is **an adult version of Where's Waldo**: spot the pattern, plot the lines, label the structure, find the setup.
- **The test:** if that's the skill, how do you improve once you can already spot and label everything correctly and you're *still* not consistently profitable?
- We're supposed to be forecasting global financial markets. **It can't be a coloring-in exercise.**

### "But I analyze every situation individually…"
- The objection: "I look at the trends, the levels, the timeframe, the confluences, then decide whether the setup is valid."
- **Look at where that process starts and ends.** It **starts with the strategy you want to apply**, then checks which features of the current chart support it. That's **adapting a predefined strategy to the situation.** You haven't analyzed the situation free of the strategy's influence.
- **Back to the production-line problem from Lesson 1:** trained to spot a known defect, you can get very sophisticated (10 criteria instead of 2, allowances for unusual variations), but **you're still inspecting for the defect you were taught to find.** You haven't learned to understand the whole system and diagnose from first principles.

### What real market analysis is
It starts **well before the setup** and goes beyond labeling and ticking boxes. In order:
1. Understand **what activity is taking place**.
2. Work out **what that tells you** about what's happening in the market.
3. See **how the pieces of information connect** in a **cause-and-effect sequence**.
4. Work out **what that reveals about the current context**.
5. Decide **which potential outcomes are genuinely meaningful**, and **what would make each more or less probable**.
6. **Only then** decide whether a trade is actually worth taking.

### The two-chart example
- Two charts, each with a **similar setup at a significant price level**.
- If analysis were just recognizing a setup and checking it meets criteria, both would lead to the same conclusion. **They don't**, because they aren't the same situation:
  - the **activity leading into** the setup is different,
  - the **overall context** leading up to that moment is different,
  - so the **meaningful potential outcomes** are different,
  - so the **information that should affect their probabilities** is different.

> **The setup doesn't contain the opportunity. The setup doesn't define the decision. The context does.**

### What technical analysis must produce
- Technical analysis **can be extremely effective, but only when you know what it has to produce.**
- **Everything you do on a chart should move you toward two things:**
  1. **identifying the meaningful potential outcomes**, and
  2. **reasoned estimates of their probabilities.**

### A self-test for your own analysis
- Can you clearly identify the **meaningful potential outcomes for each unique situation**, or are they **predefined by the rules you follow**?
- Can you identify the **relevant base rates**, and explain **which information you analyzed moves the probabilities away from them, and by how much**?
- Can you say **what new information might emerge** that would change your forecast, **how** it would change it, and **how that would change your actions**?
- If the answer is no, it doesn't mean you know nothing about analysis. It means **your current tools, techniques and skills aren't yet producing a forecast you can test and use to make a real decision.**

### Duomo's approach (briefly)
- Duomo's analysis relies on the **"Duomo market theory" and "Duomo methods"**, which he says give a route from reading the relevant market information → interpreting it → understanding the context → a **reliable, accurate forecast**. (Named, not explained, in this lesson.)

### You don't need certainty
- Your job **isn't to predict exactly what happens next** like a fortune teller.
- **Your job is to judge potential outcomes accurately enough to identify an advantage over the base rate, then take actions that reflect that advantage.**
- So whether any single trade wins or loses doesn't matter. If your forecasts are accurate enough and you keep taking those trades, it's **positive expectancy** and makes money over time because **the overall equation is in your favor.**

### Freedom from "my strategy only works on…"
- With that skill you're **not dependent on one setup appearing**, and not tied to one market or one timeframe because "that's what my strategy works on."
- **In any situation where you can analyze the chart, understand the context and identify a genuine advantage, you can produce a forecast and make a reasoned decision**: any market, any time, as long as you can do the analysis.
- That's the difference between **waiting for a setup from a predefined strategy** and **having the skill to identify an opportunity yourself.**

### What he asks you to agree on
1. **A risk-reward ratio does not, and cannot, create an advantage.**
2. **A setup can't decide what the current situation means**, and can't tell you the current context.

So **the edge has to come from your ability to form a forecast that accurately shows an advantage over the base rate.** The most valuable thing can **never be the strategy**. It has to be **your ability to make accurate judgments about the market.**

### Lead-in to Lesson 3: psychology
- If trading is judging probabilities under genuine uncertainty, think about **risking money when you can't explain the potential outcomes, estimate their probabilities, or show why the opportunity has an edge.**
- Your brain is being asked to commit money to something it **can't properly evaluate**, so it reacts as you'd expect: **hesitation, early exits, moving your stop, second-guessing every decision.**
- You've probably been told these are **discipline or psychology problems**, that emotions are running you, or that something in you needs fixing before you can trade profitably. **He says that's not right.**
- **Every trade you've ever taken was an implicit probability estimate, made without training and without a way to judge its accuracy.**
- **Lesson 3 will show** why that doesn't just damage results but changes what happens in your brain while trading, and why many so-called discipline problems are **rational responses to risking money without a justified basis for the decision.**

### Key takeaways from Part 2b
- **The ratio is set by context; on its own it means nothing.**
- **Setup-hunting ("Where's Waldo") isn't analysis**, even with many criteria, because it starts from the strategy and looks for confirmation.
- **Real analysis:** activity → meaning → cause and effect → context → meaningful outcomes → what shifts their probabilities → *then* the trade decision.
- **Same setup, different context, different decision.** The context defines the opportunity.
- **Everything on the chart should produce two things:** meaningful outcomes, and reasoned probability estimates.
- **Self-test:** outcomes per situation, base rates and how far your evidence moves them, and what new information would change the forecast and your actions.
- **No certainty needed.** You need forecasts accurate enough to beat the base rate, repeated (positive expectancy).
- **The edge is in your judgment, not the strategy.** That frees you from any single setup, market or timeframe.
- **Coming in Lesson 3:** so-called discipline and psychology problems as rational reactions to risking money without a justified forecast.
