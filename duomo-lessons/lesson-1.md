## Part 1a — lesson time 0:00 to ~5:30
Recording: `Lesson 1a.mov` (5:30)

### The opening demonstration: a great track record that means nothing
- He shows an equity curve: **just over 140% return** over the period.
  - On £10,000 capital that's £14,000 profit; on £100,000 it's £140,000.
  - It's the kind of result traders imagine would change their lives.
- He asks what you'd pay to learn that exact strategy with that verified performance. (The caption says people "happily pay $10 for far less than that in this industry" — probably a caption error for a larger figure.)
- **The catch:** the performance is real, but it was one of **200 strategies in a study, and every one of them was random**. Each was the equivalent of flipping a coin to decide whether to buy or sell.
- Plot all 200 together and they fan out into a **bell curve**: some spectacular, some disasters. That's exactly the spread you'd expect from randomness.
- So the 140% wasn't skill; it was **pure luck**. Over the next batch of the same number of trades, there's no reason it would continue. It's just as likely to be one of the terrible results.

### What that means
- Someone can show you a genuinely good track record — **real profits, verified results — and it can still be completely meaningless.**
- Over a small enough sample, randomness produces results that look exactly like skill. **You can't tell them apart by looking at the P&L.**
- He estimates around **11 million traders**, and growing. Even if every one traded randomly, there'd be plenty of lucky winners to fill social media with profit screenshots and flood comment sections under a useless strategy saying how well it works.
- So the usual ways of judging which approach is worth learning are **all contaminated by this one problem**: who's profitable, what's popular, who sounds confident, which strategy has the most screenshots, success stories and testimonials.
- The industry mostly can't tell its own luck from its skill.

### The question the series sets out to answer
> If results can't separate skill from luck, how do you build a way of trading where you can **measure whether you're actually improving**, and know whether your income is **dependable over the long term** or might just disappear?

### His background (why he cares)
- Started learning the markets in **2002**, spent many years getting it completely wrong, and was doing well enough to go **full time in 2012**. That's almost 25 years in the markets.
- Much of that first decade was spent on the same "treadmill" many traders are on: a lot of work and effort without reaching the destination.
- If you're new: this isn't something that only matters after you've wasted years. You're in the **best position to avoid it**. It's easier to build the skills from the start than to spend years attached to a strategy-first way of learning that you later have to undo.

### The root cause (his central claim)
- You were taught to **follow someone else's strategy**: look for predefined setups and apply particular rules. Maybe it's even your own strategy.
- But **nobody taught you how to analyze a unique market situation and decide what to do for yourself**, so that you actually understand what's going on.
- **The one thing to remember from the whole series:**
  > **You don't become consistently profitable by finding a strategy to follow. You become consistently profitable by developing the real skills of trading.**
- Every lesson after this one is meant to prove a different part of that sentence. By the end you should know:
  1. what those skills actually are,
  2. why learning through strategies and setups can never develop them,
  3. what your trading should look like once you've developed them properly.

### What a strategy is
- A strategy is **just a list of conditions**: if you see them, you take a particular action. **The decisions are already built in**: the setup, the entry, the stop placement, the trade-management rules.
- The "but I use discretion / I adapt it" objection: he grants that you do. But there's a **huge difference between adapting predefined rules and analyzing a genuinely unique situation from first principles.**

### The factory analogy
- Someone on a production line can get extremely good at spotting a perfect product or a known defect, maybe faster than anyone else in the building. That's a real ability.
- But it doesn't mean they understand the whole production system. They can't diagnose why an *unfamiliar* defect appeared, or redesign the process so more perfect products come out each time. **They can only spot right and wrong.**
- **That's the level most traders are taught:** recognize patterns, conditions and variations of the strategy. When the market fits a familiar category, they know what to do.
- **When it doesn't fit,** they start adding confluences, making exceptions, guessing, or sitting there hesitating to take any trade.

### What real trading skill is
Being able to face a situation you haven't seen in exactly that form before and:
1. decide **which information matters**,
2. see **how it connects**,
3. understand **what it means** and what could happen next,
4. **choose actions that follow logically** from that analysis.

A strategy can give you predefined rules, but it **can't develop that judgment**. You can't learn to decide accurately for yourself by spending your whole development following decisions that were already made for you.

### The part most traders have backwards
- Most assume **learning the skills is the long way around** and **finding the right strategy is the shortcut**. That's what everyone says.
- **It's the other way around.**
  - **Strategy-hunting never ends.** You never reach a point where performance is reliable enough to say "that's enough." There's always another strategy, setup or rule set to try. That's why people spend years feeling like they're learning while never getting the results, or the belief, to make trading a serious profession.
  - **Skill development compounds.** You build an ability, practice it, find where it's weak, correct it, and use the improved version to build the next level. Eventually you can make your own decisions, **measure the quality of those decisions**, and keep improving without anyone telling you what to do.

### Experience is not the same as skill development
- Many people think that trading long enough will give them enough experience to become successful automatically.
- Screen time **only develops your abilities when you're practicing specific trading skills and getting feedback** that shows what to correct and what you're doing right.
- Otherwise, another thousand hours at the charts can just make you **faster and more confident at repeating the same mistakes** and processes that don't produce great performance.
- He says there's a simple test to tell which of the two you've been doing. **He gives it in Lesson 4**, and warns it may be uncomfortable for some people. *(Part 1a recording ends here.)*

### Key takeaways from Part 1a
- **P&L alone can't distinguish skill from luck** on a small sample. 200 coin-flip strategies produced a 140% winner.
- **Social proof is contaminated** by survivorship among millions of traders.
- **Root cause of inconsistency:** being trained to follow pre-made decisions (strategies/setups) instead of building the judgment to analyze new situations from first principles.
- **Skill compounds; strategy-hunting doesn't.**
- **Screen time ≠ skill** unless it's deliberate practice with feedback.
- **Open thread:** the "simple test" promised in Lesson 4.
